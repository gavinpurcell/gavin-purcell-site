Explaining Why The "AI Bubble" Might Be Popping

First, a quick vocab lesson, because this whole story comes down to two terms you'll see everywhere this week.

Capex (short for capital expenditures) is the money a company spends on big physical stuff. Right now, for Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta, that mostly means AI data centers and the chips that go in them.

Free cash flow is what's left after you take the cash a business brings in and subtract that capex. It's basically the company's "money left over at the end of the month."

For twenty years, Google had more leftover money than almost any company on earth. Search ads were a money printer.

Then last Wednesday's earnings report showed that Alphabet brought in about $42 billion in cash last quarter and spent about $51 billion on capex. That left it at negative $9.1 billion in free cash flow, way worse than the roughly negative $2.7 billion Wall Street expected. It's the second quarter in a row Google has spent more than it made.

And there was a second, scarier detail. Google said that searches in its own AI Mode are eating into the old-school search ads that pay for everything (Implicator has a good breakdown).

So the thing Google is spending all this money on is starting to cannibalize the thing that pays for it.

That's a big part of what spooked investors on Thursday. On Friday, Amazon and Intel reported their own disappointing numbers and the selloff spread.

So is it a bubble?

To be completely honest, yes, I think we're in an AI investment bubble.

But that doesn't mean what a lot of people think it means.

A bubble happens when money pours into something way faster than that thing can pay it back. The technology underneath can be totally real (and life-changing!) and you can still have a bubble on top of it.

And the demand for AI is very real. Alphabet's revenue was still up 25% from last year, and Google Cloud is growing like crazy because companies can't get enough AI compute.

What Wall Street wants to know now is when all this spending pays off, and who covers the bill in the meantime. The numbers are insanely large.

TechCrunch reports that Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft plan to spend more than $740 billion on capex this year. Microsoft was the only one of the four whose cash actually covered its spending last quarter, and the biggest tech companies have borrowed about $326 billion in 2026 to keep the buildout going (three times what they borrowed last year).

We've seen this movie before (with trains)

The best comparison I've heard is the American railroad boom of the late 1800s.

Investors back then were right that railroads would change everything. So everyone rushed to build, mostly with borrowed money, and companies laid track everywhere, often right next to a competitor's line in places that didn't have enough customers for either of them.

When the Panic of 1893 hit, the money ran out, and about a quarter of all the railroad track in the US ended up in receivership (bankruptcy for railroads). The trains kept running and the tracks became the backbone of the American economy for the next century, but a whole lot of the people who paid to build them got wiped out.

Derek Thompson (him again!) did a great episode on exactly this with Liaquat Ahamed, who wrote a book about the railroad crash of 1873. Ahamed's big point: each AI company thinks it's being careful, but when everyone races to be first at the same time, you end up building way too much.

The big difference today is their actually is a LOT of money as a backstop.

Many of those railroad companies were basically one business running on loans. Google, Microsoft, Amazon and Meta have enormous money-making businesses underneath all this spending, which is why most people don't expect them to go bust.

But this week showed that backstop getting a lil thinner. Google's backstop is search, and AI is starting to eat search. And more and more of this buildout is now paid for with borrowed money, which is exactly how the railroads got into trouble.

And then there's Anthropic

The company that makes Claude filed to go public last Monday, and its numbers are honestly kind of incredible. Anthropic made $4.6 billion in revenue in all of 2025. In just the first half of 2026, it made $16.3 billion. And in the second quarter, it turned an operating profit for the first time, about $974 million.

That's the bull case for this whole bubble conversation in one company: people are actually paying for this stuff, a lot, and fast. (You'll also see a scary "$80 billion loss" in headlines, but that's mostly an accounting hit tied to the value of some of its bonds, not cash going out the door.)

The bear case is on the other side of the ledger. Anthropic has also signed on to more than half a trillion dollars in future computing deals, including roughly $114 billion with Google and $110 billion with Amazon.

Which means a lot of Big Tech's spending is a bet on companies like Anthropic, and Anthropic's spending is a bet that we all keep using more and more AI.

So the big question really is: can our use of AI grow fast enough to fill all the compute that's being built? I think it might. But nobody knows for sure, and this week Wall Street really flinched.

What all this means for you

If you mostly use ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini to get stuff done, here's what I'd keep in mind:

The tools aren't going anywhere. All of these companies are still spending more on AI next year, not less. Your chatbot isn't shutting down because a stock dropped. (The railroads kept running too!)

The cheap and free stuff might get less cheap. A lot of the AI you use right now is subsidized by exactly this spending. Meta has admitted its free Muse agent is a bit of a money pit, and when investors push for profits, that's where you usually see tighter usage limits, more ads or higher prices.

My wife has gotten very into Instinct, the (totally free!) AI agent that lives in iMessage and does a LOT of stuff for you. I'd bet that won't be free much longer.

Your retirement account is probably in this too. These companies make up a huge chunk of the big index funds. Not financial advice (we are just guys on a podcast), but it's worth knowing that "the AI trade" isn't just a Silicon Valley thing anymore.

Who Actually Gets Rich From AI?

Investor Will Manidis (with co-author Joe Roberts) spent a year writing a massive essay called The Commanding Heights, and it went super viral this week (2M views on his launch post).

See the post from @WillManidis on X

It's LONG, so here's the tl;dr.

The big idea is that new technologies almost never make the most money for the people who invent them. Railroad fortunes mostly went to people who never built a train. He thinks AI will go the same way.

The models themselves get cheaper and more alike every few months (he calls intelligence "the giveaway, a loss leader"), so the real money goes to whoever controls the chokepoints that everything else has to pass through: the payment rails, the infrastructure and, increasingly, governments.

It ties right into the bubble story above. He even predicts big tech's cash flow gets squeezed in 2027 before things recover. There's also a darker thread about jobs, especially white-collar work outside the US, so def go in with your eyes open.

We 💛 This: AI Doc on How AI Educates

The third film in our Imogen series is here, and it's the biggest one yet. It's about 36 minutes long and, like the others, it was made with AI (more on that in a sec).

This time the film follows Jamie, a fictional 13-year-old, through a full school year as she tries to write one assignment: "What do you want to be when you grow up?" The twist is that the narrator is the AI the kids are using to cheat.

It digs into real stuff happening in schools right now, like the case of Orion Newby, a college freshman at Adelphi University whose history paper got flagged by Turnitin as 100% AI-generated.
